Volume II: History — Chapter 8: History of the Guilds

"A guild is not a job. It is family, it is school, it is court. The guild gives you a name and takes your name when you depart. The guild is everything." — From the Codex of Guilds, +1,500

Related sections: → see [Volume II, Ch.06, "The Era of Unity"], → see [Volume I, Ch.08, "Culture and Society"], → see [Volume II, Ch.09, "Economic History"] Era: −2,000 – present Length: 30 pages

Introduction

The guilds are a unique social institution of Xha'Ruun civilization with no direct analogue in the history of other known civilizations. It is not a trade union or a corporation in the earthly sense: a guild is simultaneously a school, a family, a court, and a social network. It gives a person professional identity, a name in society, and protection — from training to old age.

This chapter traces the path of the guild system from the craft associations of the First Empire to the twelve guilds of the present that manage the key spheres of the civilization.

8.1 Origin of the Guilds

The guild system arose in the First Empire as associations of craftsmen by profession. The workshops controlled product quality, the monopoly on craft, and the transfer of mastery.

The decisive turn came after the Empire's fall (−2,150): in the dark ages it was precisely the workshops that became centers for the preservation of knowledge and professional skills. When the imperial institutions collapsed, the workshops and their corporations remained the only structure capable of maintaining the continuity of craft. Thus the guilds acquired functions extending far beyond economics: they became keepers of knowledge in the literal sense (→ see [Volume II, Ch.04, "The Era of Crisis"]).

8.2 Formation of the System

Period Development
−6,150 – −2,150 Craft associations (First Empire)
−2,150 – −750 Guilds as centers of knowledge
−750 – +850 Guilds as economic unions
+850 – +1,500 Formal recognition by the Council of Unity
+1,500 – present Modern structure (12 guilds)

Each stage added new functions to the guilds. In the recovery era (−750 – +850) they became economic unions controlling trade and production. With the founding of the Council of Unity (+850) the guild system received official recognition — and simultaneously political representation: 24 of 49 seats in the Council were assigned to the guilds.

8.4 Functions of a Guild

A guild performs four basic functions:

  1. Education — professional training through the "apprentice → journeyman → master" system. Training lasts years and includes not only the craft but the ethics of the profession;
  2. Court — internal jurisdiction over members: professional disputes, quality standards, discipline;
  3. Social protection — support of members in illness, old age, and adversity; the guild "accepts the name" and accompanies to the end of life;
  4. Representation — delegation of members to the Guild Chamber of the Council of Unity.

This fullness of functions makes the guild for the Xha'Ruun not a "place of work" but an institution of socialization in the full sense — hence the chapter's epigraph: "The guild is everything."

8.5 The Guild and the State

The relationship between guilds and the state (the Council of Unity) is built on the principle of mutual control:

  • The guilds delegate to the Council 24 of 49 members;
  • The regions (domains) delegate 25 members — a balance that prevents either side from dominating;
  • Key decisions require the consent of both chambers;
  • The Keeper of Knowledge controls the conformity of decisions to the canon.

This balance is a deliberate institutional choice: the guilds represent the horizontal (professional) cross-section of society, the regions — the vertical (territorial) one. Their equilibrium ensured three thousand years of political stability (→ see [Volume II, Ch.06, "The Era of Unity"]).

8.6 Influence and Criticism

The guild system is not free of contradictions:

  • Risk of corporate closure — guilds can become closed elites restricting access;
  • Conservatism of standards — quality control sometimes hampers innovation;
  • Distribution of influence — the largest guilds (Agronomists, 5.6 million; Merchants, 4.2 million) carry more political weight than the small ones (Scribes, 0.5 million; Warriors, 0.6 million).

Modern practice mitigates these risks through mechanisms of external audit, open access to training, and rotation of delegates. Nonetheless, the discussion of the guilds' place in society remains a living topic of political thought.

  5. The guild-region balance (24 + 25) — the foundation of political stability.
  6. Criticism of the system: risks of closure, conservatism, and inequality of influence.
