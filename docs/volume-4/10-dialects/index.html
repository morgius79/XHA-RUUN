Volume IV: Language — Chapter 10: Dialects

"Three continents — three accents. Kharak — a hard 'r', Rhuval — a melodious 'a', Vexar — a breathy 'h'. The language is one, but the voices are different." — Phonetic Atlas, +3 200 Y.E.

Introduction

The Xha'Ruun language is one across the entire planet, but dialectal differences reflect the history and geography of the domains (→ see [Volume III, Ch.05, "The 8 Domains"]). The dialects are mutually intelligible — a result of the centralization of writing and education — yet they preserve phonetic and lexical features.

10.1 The Main Dialects

Dialect Continent Speakers Distinguishing Features
Kharak-Thar Kharak 1.2 billion Standard
Kharak-Tor Kharak 0.6 billion Hard "g," glottal stop
Rhuval-Ven Rhuval 0.8 billion Melodious "a," elongated vowels
Vexar-Shan Vexar 0.4 billion Breathy "h," nasal vowels
Island Islands 0.3 billion Archaic vocabulary

The standard dialect is Kharak-Thar — the language of the capital and the academies. The largest number of speakers belongs to the standard dialect (1.2 billion).

10.2 Mutual Intelligibility

All dialects are mutually intelligible. The differences are at the level of pronunciation and up to 5% of the lexicon.

Mutual intelligibility is ensured by:

  • A unified writing system — Sharuu does not reflect differences in pronunciation;
  • Centralized education — the standard is taught everywhere;
  • Mass media — standard speech on the air.

10.3 Phonetic Features

The dialectal differences affect:

  • Kharak-Tor — velarization and the glottal stop ʔ;
  • Rhuval-Ven — vowel elongation, "melodiousness";
  • Vexar-Shan — devoicing of h, nasalization;
  • Island — archaic tones.

These features trace back to the history of the settlement of the continents (→ see [Volume II, Ch.01, "The Pre-Civilization Era"]).

10.4 Lexical Differences

Lexical differences (up to 5%) concern:

  • Natural terms — local flora and fauna;
  • Economic terms — the specialization of the domains;
  • Borrowings — from the languages of the Kel'vash and the Moryn.

10.5 Dialects and Identity

A dialect is a marker of domain identity (→ see [Volume III, Ch.05]). The use of a dialect in everyday speech is combined with the standard in official contexts — "unity without unification" in language.

Chapter Summary

  1. Five main dialects; the standard is Kharak-Thar.
  2. All dialects are mutually intelligible (up to 5% of the lexicon).
  3. Phonetic differences: velarization, elongation, nasalization, archaic tones.
  4. Mutual intelligibility — writing, education, media.
  5. A dialect is a marker of domain identity.
