Volume III: Society — Chapter 4: The 12 Guilds: Structure

"Joining a Guild is being born anew. You receive a name, a profession, a family. You receive a purpose." — Guild Charter, +1,500

Related sections: → see [Volume III, Ch.01, "System of Government"], → see [Volume II, Ch.08, "History of the Guilds"], → see [Volume III, Ch.03, "Economic System"] Length: 30 pages

Introduction

The twelve guilds are the heart of Xha'Ruun social organization. They are not trade unions or corporations: a guild is simultaneously a school, a family, a court, and a social network. This chapter describes the internal structure of the guilds — their principles, hierarchy, functions, and the member's life cycle.

4.1 Principles of the Guilds

Principle Description
Voluntariness Entry is voluntary
Meritocracy Advancement by ability
Mutual aid The guild supports its members
Autonomy Internal affairs without Council interference

Voluntariness distinguishes the guilds from the state in the traditional sense: membership is not mandatory, but for most professions it is a practical necessity — the guild controls access to education, licenses, and resources.

The largest guild — Agronomists (5.6 million); the smallest — Scribes (0.5 million). Size reflects the sector's scale, not political weight: the Keeper Guild (0.8 million) holds a constitutional function.

4.3 Internal Hierarchy

The guild hierarchy — "apprentice → journeyman → master":

Level Status Rights
Apprentice Beginner Training, without vote
Journeyman Certified Work, attendance at meetings
Master Highest Teaching apprentices, voting
Elder Lifelong Guild leadership

Advancement — by trial results and contribution (meritocracy), not by seniority.

4.4 Functions of a Guild

  1. Education — professional training through workshops and academies;
  2. Court — internal jurisdiction on professional matters;
  3. Social protection — support in illness, old age, adversity;
  4. Representation — delegating members to the Council of Unity;
  5. Standards — quality control and sector norms.

4.5 The Member's Life Cycle

  • Entry — after a probationary period and oath;
  • Name — the guild "gives a name" (professional identity);
  • Career — training, trials, advancement;
  • Departure — the guild "takes the name" and accompanies the member.

The guild accompanies a person from training to old age — hence the epigraph: "The guild is everything."

4.6 Guilds and the Council

Each guild delegates 2 members to the Guild Chamber of the Council of Unity (→ see [Volume III, Ch.02, "Council of Unity"]). Guilds' internal affairs are autonomous, but their charters cannot contradict the canon — controlled by the Keeper Guild.

Chapter Summary

  1. Four principles: voluntariness, meritocracy, mutual aid, autonomy.
  2. 12 guilds, ~25 million members, from 0.5 to 5.6 million.
  3. Hierarchy "apprentice → journeyman → master → elder".
  4. Five functions: education, court, social protection, representation, standards.
  5. The guild accompanies a member from entry to departure.
  6. Guild autonomy is limited by the canon (Keeper control).
