## Introduction

Evidentiality is the most striking grammatical phenomenon of the Xha'Ruun language. Every utterance obliges the speaker to indicate the source of information: direct observation, indirect perception, inference, or retelling. The language does not allow the origin of knowledge to be concealed — a reflection of a culture in which truth is inseparable from its source.

---

## 6.1 Four Types of Evidentiality

| Type | Suffix | Meaning | Example |
|------|--------|---------|---------|
| Direct | -ven | I see/hear it myself | **khal-en-ven** — "does (I see)" |
| Indirect | -sha | I feel/hear | **khal-en-sha** — "does (I hear)" |
| Inferential | -tor | I draw a conclusion | **khal-en-tor** — "must be doing" |
| Reportative | -rass | I was told | **khal-en-rass** — "they say (he) does" |

The distinction between direct (-ven) and indirect (-sha) is sensory modality: visual versus auditory/tactile. The inferential (-tor) marks inference from evidence; the reportative (-rass) — the retelling of another's speech.

---

## 6.2 Obligatoriness

Evidentiality is mandatory in every utterance. Omitting evidentiality is a grammatical error.

It marks not only verbs, but also nominal predicates, subordinate clauses, and even individual particles. In connected texts, the reportative is regularly used for citation, making the boundary between direct and reported speech explicit.

---

## 6.3 Evidentiality and Grammar

Evidentiality interacts with other categories:

- **With tense** — the inferential in the past tense marks a retrospective inference;
- **With negation** — negation may fall within the scope of evidentiality ("I see that it is not...");
- **With interrogativity** — in questions, evidentiality indicates the expected source of the answer.

---

## 6.4 Cultural Significance

Evidentiality is not only grammar, but also a cultural norm:

- **Science** — requires an explicit indication of the source of data;
- **Law** — distinguishes eyewitness testimony from retelling;
- **Religion** — sacred texts are marked by direct evidentiality ("I see") or reportative ("transmitted").

Language reflects and shapes culture: the obligatoriness of the source of knowledge is a principle built into speech itself.
